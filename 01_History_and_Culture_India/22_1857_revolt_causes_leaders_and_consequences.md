# The Revolt of 1857: Causes, Key Leaders, Suppression & Aftermath


The Great Uprising of 1857 was South Asia's first widespread armed rebellion against British East India Company rule. Sparked by sepoy disaffection over the greased Enfield cartridges at Barrackpore and Meerut, the revolt quickly enveloped northern and central India, uniting dispossessed royal houses, peasants, and sepoys under the nominal banner of Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar.

## 2. Salient Dimensions & Critical Analysis

* Underlying Causes: Political (Doctrine of Lapse, Annexation of Awadh 1856, pension termination of Nana Saheb); Economic (ruin of peasants under heavy land revenue, destruction of artisans); Religious-Social (General Service Enlistment Act 1856 requiring overseas service, Christian missionary proselytization, Religious Disabilities Act 1856); Immediate trigger: introduction of Enfield rifle greased with cow and pig fat.

* Mangal Pandey (29 March 1857): Sepoy of 34th Bengal Native Infantry at Barrackpore who shot British officers Baugh and Hearsey; hanged on 8 April 1857.

* Reasons for Failure: Limited geographical scope (mostly North and Central India; South, West, and Punjab remained largely peaceful); absence of modern unified ideology; lack of support from educated middle class and princely rulers (Scindia, Holkar, Nizam aided British - Lord Canning called them 'breakwaters to the storm'); superior British logistics, arms, and electric telegraph communication.

* Constitutional Aftermath: Government of India Act 1858 (ended EIC rule; Crown assumed direct sovereignty via Secretary of State for India and Viceroy); Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1 Nov 1858) promising non-annexation of princely states; Peale Commission reorganized Indian Army (ratio of European to Indian troops raised to 1:2 in Bengal, divide-and-rule 'martial race' theory).

### Part B: Screening / Prelims Objective Questions

* Q1. Which heroic sepoy of the 34th Bengal Native Infantry fired at his British commanding officers at Barrackpore on 29 March 1857, igniting the 1857 uprising?
(a) Bakht Khan
(b) Mangal Pandey
(c) Ishwar Pandey
(d) Mata Din
Correct Answer: (b) Mangal Pandey [Explanation: Sepoy Mangal Pandey of the 34th BNI revolted at Barrackpore parade ground on 29 March 1857 protesting greased cartridges, becoming the opening martyr of the revolt.]

* Q2. The octogenarian Rajput zamindar who led the 1857 armed resistance in Jagdishpur (Arrah, Bihar) and inflicted repeated defeats on British forces was:
(a) Amar Singh
(b) Kunwar Singh
(c) Pir Ali
(d) Khan Bahadur Khan
Correct Answer: (b) Kunwar Singh [Explanation: Babu Kunwar Singh, the 80-year-old chieftain of Jagdishpur in Bihar, displayed extraordinary military leadership and guerrilla warfare against British detachments.]

* Q3. British commander Sir Hugh Rose, after capturing Gwalior, paid tribute to which rebel leader as 'the only man among the rebels' and 'the bravest and best military leader'?
(a) Begum Hazrat Mahal
(b) Rani Lakshmibai
(c) Tatya Tope
(d) Nana Saheb
Correct Answer: (b) Rani Lakshmibai [Explanation: General Sir Hugh Rose, who commanded the Central India Field Force, lauded Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi for her unparalleled valor after she fell in battle at Kotah-ki-Serai.]

* Q4. Which British monarchial declaration, read out by Lord Canning at the Allahabad Darbar on 1 November 1858, announced the direct assumption of Indian governance by the British Crown?
(a) Charter Act of 1858
(b) Queen Victoria's Proclamation
(c) Royal Titles Act
(d) Government of India Manifesto
Correct Answer: (b) Queen Victoria's Proclamation [Explanation: Lord Canning read Queen Victoria's Proclamation at Allahabad on 1 November 1858, guaranteeing respect for ancient rights and pledging no further territorial annexations.]

* Q5. Which commission was constituted immediately after the 1857 Revolt to reorganize the Indian Army and ensure higher ratios of European troops?
(a) Hunter Commission
(b) Peale Commission
(c) Fraser Commission
(d) Welby Commission
Correct Answer: (b) Peale Commission [Explanation: The Peale Commission (1858) recommended maintaining a strict 1:2 European-to-Indian soldier ratio in Bengal and 1:3 in Madras/Bombay, pioneering the 'martial vs non-martial' recruitment doctrine.]
